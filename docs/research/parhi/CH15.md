## 1. Concepts, in our words

### 15.1–15.2 Subexpression elimination (pp. 559–560)

- Constant multiplications become shifts and adds. When several constants multiply the **same** variable, their
  shift-and-add forms often contain the same partial sums; compute those once and share them (subexpression
  elimination, also called substructure sharing). Example: 13·x and 27·x share `x + (x << 3)`: 3 shifts and 3 adds
  instead of 5 and 5 (p. 560).

### 15.3 Multiple constant multiplication (MCM) (pp. 560–566)

- An iterative matching algorithm (p. 561): write the constants in binary, count bit-wise matches between all pairs,
  take the best match, remove it as a shared term and put the remainders back, repeat while it pays. Example: 237, 182
  and 93 need 9 shifts and 9 adds instead of 14 and 13 (p. 562). Savings grow with word length and with the number of
  constants.
- **Linear transformations y = T·x with a constant matrix** (pp. 562–564): apply MCM to each column (constants that
  multiply the same x_j), form the unique products, then share additions between outputs by matching which products
  each output needs. Example: a 4 × 4 matrix goes from 35 to 20 additions.
- **Polynomial evaluation** (pp. 565–566): share powers through the binary form of the exponents; one polynomial needs
  6 multiplications instead of 22; several polynomials in one variable share both powers and coefficient terms.

### 15.4 Sharing in FIR filters (pp. 566–573)

- In the transposed (data-broadcast) FIR form one input multiplies all coefficients, so MCM applies across taps. A table
  with taps as rows and shifts as columns exposes subexpressions, also across delays; a subexpression of size m that
  occurs n times is equivalent to one of size n occurring m times (p. 568). Examples: 3-tap filters drop from 12 shifts
  and 11 adds to 7 shifts and 9 adds, or to 8 adds with another sharing (p. 570).
- With CSD coefficients the two most common subexpressions are `x + (x >> 2)` (pattern 101) and `x − (x >> 2)` (pattern
  10−1). Building every coefficient from these two and single digits cuts the number of terms by about a third against
  the number of nonzero digits (p. 571); three shared buses feed one row of adders, and the three data paths can be
  balanced by departing slightly from strict CSD (pp. 571–573).

### 15.5 Number splitting (pp. 574–583)

- **Additive, row-based**: within one output, split a coefficient into a copy of another one plus a small remainder, so
  that one multiplier is shared (a coefficient can even become a simple shift, e.g. 0.7 → 0.5 + 0.2). **Column-based**: a
  product used by several outputs is computed once. **Multiplicative**: write T as a product T1·T2·T3 using paired row and
  column operations that leave the product unchanged, steering toward trivial (shift) coefficients; an example drops from
  9 nontrivial multiplications to 2. All are greedy searches guided by an area or power cost.

### 15.6–15.7 Conclusions and problems (pp. 583–590)

- Used for fixed-coefficient FIR, IIR, multirate and wavelet filters; the same idea as common-subexpression elimination
  in compilers. Problems include MCM sets, linear transforms, a multiplierless YUV-to-RGB converter, bit-serial filters
  and number splitting.

## 3. What this means for SENTINEL

1. **The checksum-weight generator is exactly a constant linear transformation** [INFERENCE, checked numerically].
   `(wc1, wc2, wc3)ᵀ = T·w` with the constant 3 × 8 matrix `T = [1 … 1; 1 2 … 8; 1 4 … 64]` (`docs/SPEC.md` 6.1). Section
   15.3.1 is the textbook method for it: column-wise MCM (each w_k is multiplied by {1, k, k²}), then shared additions
   across the three outputs. Two exact integer identities give sharing for free, and both were checked on 1000 random int8
   weight vectors:
   - per column, `k²·w_k = k·(k·w_k)`, so the C3 term reuses the C2 term (saves one adder in columns 5 and 7);
   - with suffix sums `S_j = Σ_{k≥j} w_k`: `wc1 = S_1`, `wc2 = Σ_j S_j`, `wc3 = Σ_j (2j − 1)·S_j`.
   This turns the `CH13.md` candidate (a flat CSD multi-operand tree) into a small design space: minimum depth (one flat
   tree of 14 shifted terms for wc3) versus minimum adders (shared terms). The worst path today is depth-limited
   (`CH02.md`, 10 logic levels, [VIVADO-STA]), so depth comes first; the sharing is second priority. Either way the outputs
   are the same integers, so bit-exactness is not at stake.
2. **No sharing in the lanes.** SENTINEL's broadcast activation multiplies K *variable* weights, not constants, so the MCM
   and FIR-sharing methods of this chapter do not apply to the data lanes.
3. **Number splitting has no target here.** It works on constant matrices; apart from the checksum matrix above, SENTINEL's
   coefficients are trained weights loaded at run time.
