## 1. Concepts, in our words

### 12.1 Why lattice filters (pp. 421–422)

- Direct-form IIR filters are very sensitive to coefficient quantization (polynomial roots move a lot), especially with
  crowded poles. Cascade and parallel forms help, but not for narrow-band filters with poles at small angles. Lattice
  filters synthesise the denominator robustly and are a cascade of identical modules, which suits VLSI.

### 12.2 The Schur algorithm (pp. 422–429)

- From a stable denominator, the Schur algorithm builds a sequence of polynomials of falling degree by a degree-reduction
  step with parameter k_i; the denominator is stable exactly when every step keeps the constant term smaller than the
  leading coefficient. The step can be written with 2 × 2 determinants (eq. 12.6), which later helps pipelining.
- With an inner product defined by the average power at an internal node for white-noise input (eq. 12.10), the Schur
  polynomials are orthonormal. Any polynomial can be expanded in them (polynomial expansion algorithm), and the power at a
  node is then the sum of the squared expansion coefficients (eq. 12.35).

### 12.3–12.6 Four lattice structures (pp. 429–454)

- **Basic** lattice (two multipliers per module): denominator from the k-parameters, numerator from tap gains given by the
  expansion; node powers can differ widely when k-parameters are near 1, forcing input scaling and more roundoff noise.
  An FIR lattice is also derived (2N multipliers, not efficient as a plain FIR filter, but useful for adaptive filters).
- **One-multiplier** lattice: sign parameters are chosen so node powers stay as large as possible without exceeding the
  largest one, which reduces input scaling.
- **Normalized** lattice: each module is a Givens rotation; every node of the feedback part has unit power, so the all-pole
  part is orthonormal (K = I).
- **Scaled-normalized** lattice: slow-down and retiming (chapter 11) turn every node into a state, then diagonal scaling gives
  unit power everywhere without input scaling. The book cites a simulation in which a normalized lattice needed about 10 bits
  fewer than a parallel form for a band-pass filter with clustered poles (p. 450).
- The book notes that multiplier hardware grows with the square of the word length, so a structure with more multipliers but
  shorter words can be cheaper than one with fewer, longer multipliers (p. 450).

### 12.7 Roundoff noise (pp. 454–458)

- Noise gains from internal nodes to the output follow from the transposed graph and the polynomial expansion. Taking the tap
  gains from the Schur-polynomial side gives less noise than from the reverse side (an elliptic example: 52.8 % of the NSR).

### 12.8–12.10 Pipelining and power (pp. 458–469)

- If the denominator has M − 1 zero coefficients between nonzero ones (a polynomial in z^M, as after scattered look-ahead), the
  Schur algorithm gives M − 1 consecutive zero k-parameters, so each loop holds M delays and the lattice can be pipelined M
  levels (zero-coefficient and zero-constant-term properties, pp. 459–460). Constrained filter design reaches this without
  cancelling zeros and with far fewer multiplications than scattered look-ahead (Table 12.1). Example: a 4-stage pipelined
  scaled-normalized lattice at a reduced supply uses 24.14 % of the power for 70 % more area (p. 469).

### 12.11–12.12 Conclusions and problems (pp. 470–476)

- Problems: Schur bases, the four structures, pipelined forms, noise, all-pass decomposition (pole interlacing) and
  "stretched" narrow-band designs.

## 3. What this means for SENTINEL

1. **Nothing to build from this chapter.** SENTINEL contains no IIR or lattice filters.
2. **Multiplier cost grows with the square of the word length** (p. 450). This matters for the Winograd-type option of
   `CH08.md` [INFERENCE]: if the transformed operands grow from 8 × 8 to about 11 × 12 bits, a LUT multiplier grows by roughly
   (11·12)/(8·8) ≈ 2.1 times, which eats most of the 2.25× saving in multiplications. The gain would then be in cycles, not in
   LUTs, unless the multipliers go to DSP slices, whose cost per multiplier does not grow with operand width inside the
   slice's native multiplier size. That size (commonly quoted as 25 × 18 bits) is [UNKNOWN] in this repository until it is
   read in DS180 (`docs/vendor/amd/ds180/`); check it before relying on it.
3. **A loose analogy for the report.** The scaled-normalized lattice gives every internal node the same power so one word
   length fits all. SENTINEL's per-layer requant parameters (M, n; `docs/SPEC.md` 3.3 and 4) do the same job for a CNN: each
   layer's output is rescaled to fill the 8-bit range. This is an explanation, not a technique to add.
