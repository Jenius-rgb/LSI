## 1. Concepts, in our words

### 11.1 Two quantization effects (pp. 377–378)

- Coefficient quantization changes the transfer function itself. Signal rounding inside the filter causes limit
  cycles (periodic outputs, possible when a nonlinearity sits in a feedback loop) and roundoff noise (a random error
  that linear theory can model). Internal overflow causes much larger errors and is prevented by scaling.

### 11.2 Scaling and roundoff noise (pp. 378–382)

- **Scaling** moves gain around without changing the transfer function: divide the path into a node by β and multiply
  the path out of it by β.
  - l1 scaling: `β = Σ|f(i)|` (eq. 11.2), f the impulse response from the input to the node. With inputs bounded by 1,
    overflow is impossible; the strictest rule.
  - l2 scaling: `β = δ·√(Σ f²(i))` (eq. 11.3), based on the variance for white-noise input; overflow is possible but
    rarer for larger δ. Larger δ costs signal-to-noise ratio: overflow and roundoff noise trade against each other.
- **Roundoff noise**: the product of two W-bit fractions has 2W − 1 bits and must be rounded back. Modelled as an added
  white noise, uniform over one quantization step, uncorrelated with everything else; its variance is
  `σ_e² = 2^(−2W)/3` (eq. 11.9), so each extra bit cuts the noise power by 4. The book assumes a double-length
  accumulator: products are summed at full width and rounded once.

### 11.3 State-variable description (pp. 382–385)

- `x(n+1) = A·x(n) + b·u(n)`, `y(n) = cᵀ·x(n) + d·u(n)`. The state covariance `K = Σ_k A^k b bᵀ (Aᵀ)^k` satisfies the
  Lyapunov equation `K = A·K·Aᵀ + b·bᵀ` (eq. 11.25); the output covariance `W = Aᵀ·W·A + c·cᵀ` (eq. 11.28). If all
  states have the same power, one word length fits all; an orthogonal structure (K = I) is scaled automatically, has
  low roundoff noise and cannot oscillate from overflow.

### 11.4 Computing scaling and noise (pp. 386–391)

- A diagonal transformation with `t_ii = √K_ii` gives every state unit power. Total output roundoff noise is
  `σ_e²·trace(W)`, and for the scaled system `σ_e²·Σ K_ii·W_ii` (eq. 11.48), so the noise of the scaled filter can be
  found from the unscaled K and W. Nodes without rounding are left out of the sum. Scaling equalises word lengths; it
  does not reduce noise, and can increase it (Example 11.4.2).

### 11.5 Pipelined IIR filters (pp. 391–402)

- For first- and second-order filters pipelined by scattered look-ahead (chapter 10), closed forms for the roundoff noise
  and noise-to-signal ratio are derived for power-of-2 levels M. Noise grows sharply as poles approach the unit circle;
  for poles near 1, scaled noise falls with M while unscaled noise stays about constant (Table 11.2).

### 11.6–11.7 Fast computation and the SRP transformation (pp. 403–410)

- K and W can be computed by a doubling iteration (F ← A, K ← b·bᵀ; repeat K ← F·K·Fᵀ + K, F ← F²), which converges
  for stable filters. Nodes that are not states (not at a delay) cannot be scaled this way; slow-down, retiming and
  pipelining (SRP) move delays so that every rounding node becomes a state, without changing finite-word-length
  behaviour (pp. 405–408). Examples: a scaled-normalized lattice and an 8-tap broadcast FIR filter (total scaled noise
  1.835 σ_e²).

### 11.8–11.9 Conclusions and problems (pp. 410–419)

- Problems: noise of all-pole filters near the unit circle, l1 scaling with power-of-2 scale factors, pipelined
  structures, and low-noise parallel FIR filters.

## 3. What this means for SENTINEL

1. **SENTINEL has no roundoff noise in the book's sense inside the engine.** All products and sums are exact integers;
   the only rounding is the requant step `(acc·M) >> n` with rounding at .5 and saturation (`docs/SPEC.md` 3.3), and the
   golden model does exactly the same. The book's "double-length accumulator, round once" model is what SENTINEL
   does. The difference between the integer and the float model is an accuracy question, measured in
   `results/model/<m>/quant_result.json` [SIM], not a hardware one.
2. **SENTINEL uses l1-type scaling.** The exporter asserts worst-case bounds per layer for the actual weights
   (`check_bit_bounds` in `model/export.py`, `docs/SPEC.md` 3.6): `|acc| ≤ 2^(ACC_W−1) − 1`, the checksum accumulators
   against ACC_CS_W, and the requant product against its width. That is the strict rule of eq. 11.2: no overflow can
   occur. l2 scaling would allow narrower words at a risk of overflow, which a bit-exact, self-checking design should
   not take.
3. **Narrower accumulators are possible but buy little** [INFERENCE]. With `ACC_W = 32` and `ACC_CS_W = 48`
   (`config/hw_default.yaml`), the accumulators have headroom: for any int8 weights and uint8 activations the sum of
   T products is at most `T·127·255` (e.g. T = 512 inputs gives 16,581,120, a 25-bit signed value), before the bias.
   A narrower ACC_W would save flip-flops and shorten the accumulator carry chain, but it must still cover the bias range
   and every model that may be loaded over SPI without resynthesis, and the accumulator is not on the critical path
   (`CH02.md`). Not worth the risk before the freeze.
4. **Requant error, for the report.** The requant rounding error is at most half an output step; under the book's
   uniform model its variance is 1/12 of a step squared. This is a way to explain the small accuracy loss of the integer
   model, labelled [SIM] with the accuracy figures it explains.
5. **Limit cycles cannot occur**: there are no recursive filters, and the accumulators restart for every unit.
