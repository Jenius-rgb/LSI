## 1. Concepts, in our words

### 9.1 Why (pp. 255–256)

- Plain parallel processing multiplies the area by the block size L. Strength reduction with substructure sharing
  gives parallel structures whose area grows less than linearly: parallel FIR filters, fast DCTs, and parallel
  rank-order filters.

### 9.2 Parallel FIR filters (pp. 256–275)

- **Polyphase form** (pp. 256–260): split input, filter and output into L phases. An L-parallel FIR filter is then
  L² subfilters of length N/L, i.e. L·N multiplications, linear in L; the subfilter matrix is pseudocirculant.
- **Fast FIR algorithms (FFA)** (pp. 260–264): two polynomials of degree L − 1 can be multiplied with 2L − 1
  products, so about 2L − 1 subfilters of length N/L suffice, i.e. about `2N − N/L` multiplications (p. 260).
  - 2-parallel: `Y0 = H0·X0 + z⁻²·H1·X1`, `Y1 = (H0 + H1)(X0 + X1) − H0·X0 − H1·X1` (eq. 9.18): 3 subfilters,
    1.5N multiplications and 1.5N + 1 additions instead of 2N and 2(N − 1). N = 8: 12 multiplications and 13
    additions instead of 16 and 14.
  - 3-parallel (applying the 2-parallel idea recursively): 6 subfilters, 2N multiplications and 2N + 4 additions,
    about 33 % less than 3N.
  - In matrix form `Y = Q·H·P·X` (post-processing, diagonal of subfilters, pre-processing). Other equivalent
    structures use H0 − H1 instead of H0 + H1; which one needs fewer nonzero coefficient bits depends on the
    filter's spectrum (p. 262).
- **Transposition** (pp. 264–267): transposing an FFA, or transposing a fast linear convolution of chapter 8, gives
  another parallel filter with the same operation count but different finite-word-length behaviour.
- **Large block sizes by cascading** (pp. 268–275): an m-parallel FFA followed by an n-parallel FFA gives an
  (m·n)-parallel filter; multiplications `Π M_i · N / Π L_i` (eq. 9.35), additions by eq. 9.36. 4-parallel (2 × 2):
  9N/4 multiplications, about 44 % saving; 6-parallel (2 then 3): 3N, about 50 %; 8-parallel (2 × 2 × 2): 27N/8,
  about 58 %. Apply the 2-parallel FFA first (fewer additions, Example 9.2.1). The tensor (Kronecker) product
  writes the cascades compactly.
- Uses (p. 275): wavelets and subband coders, adaptive equalizers, 2D parallel FIR filters, motion estimation.
  Problem 23: a 2D filter computed in 2 × 2 output blocks with the 2-parallel FFA in both directions needs 9N²/16
  multiplications per output instead of N².

### 9.3 DCT and IDCT (pp. 275–285)

- Direct N-point DCT: N(N − 1) multiplications. Algorithm–architecture mapping turns the 8-point DCT from 56 into
  13 multiplications (11 for a scaled DCT) using rotator blocks whose angles add when cascaded (Example 9.3.1).
- Decimation in frequency splits an N-point IDCT into two N/2-point ones, down to 2-point butterflies; about
  (N/2)·log₂N multiplications; the 8-point fast IDCT needs 13 (p. 285). The DCT is the transpose of the IDCT.

### 9.4 Parallel rank-order filters (pp. 285–296)

- Rank-order filters sort a sliding window. Batcher's odd–even merge-sort builds sorters from compare-and-swap (C&S)
  units. In parallel versions, comparisons shared between neighbouring outputs are done once: window 5 with
  L = 2 needs 13 C&S instead of 18; L = 3, 21 instead of 27; window 7 with L = 4, 49 instead of 64. A time-mapping
  ("running order") sorter reuses merge units across time steps (window 8: 13 instead of 19 C&S).
- Because area grows less than linearly, power can keep falling with L even after the supply voltage reaches its
  floor (eq. 9.77).

### 9.5–9.6 Conclusions and problems (pp. 297–311)

- Problems include difference coefficients for narrow-band filters and difference inputs for slowly varying
  signals (fewer nonzero bits, problems 20–21), low-power VQ by algorithm choice (problem 22), and the fast 2D
  filter (problem 23).

## 3. What this means for SENTINEL

1. **FFA versus Winograd for 3 × 3 layers** [INFERENCE, our count]. Applying the 2-parallel FFA to a 3-tap filter
   gives subfilters H0 = {h0, h2}, H1 = {h1} and H0 + H1 = {h0 + h1, h2}: 5 multiplications per 2 outputs instead of 6.
   In both directions (tensor product, as in problem 23) that is 25 multiplications per 2 × 2 output window and
   input channel instead of 36, about 1.44 times fewer. The Winograd-type tile of `CH08.md` needs 16 (2.25 times
   fewer). For SENTINEL's 3 × 3 kernels the chapter 8 route is the stronger one; the FFA's advantage shows on long
   filters, which SENTINEL does not have.
2. **Substructure sharing in the checksum-weight generator** [INFERENCE]. `wc1 = Σ w_k`, `wc2 = Σ k·w_k` and
   `wc3 = Σ k²·w_k` (`docs/SPEC.md` 6.1) can share partial sums instead of three separate trees. One way: suffix
   sums `S_j = Σ_{k≥j} w_k` give `wc1 = S_1` and `wc2 = Σ_j S_j`, and wc3 follows with odd-number weights on S_j.
   This is the "share common terms" idea of this chapter; whether it shortens the worst path (the C3 tree,
   `CH02.md`) or only saves LUTs needs a synthesis run. Chapter 13 (CSD, constant multiplication) and chapter 15
   are the more direct tools for the constant weights k and k²; to be weighed there.
3. **Max-pool and argmax are rank-order operations.** A 2 × 2 max-pool needs 3 comparisons and the 10-class argmax 9;
   for a single maximum there is nothing to share, so section 9.4 gives no saving here.
4. **Equivalent structures with different word-length behaviour** (pp. 262, 264). For SENTINEL this is not a risk:
   all arithmetic is integer and exact, so any transposed or reordered structure gives identical results as long as
   every intermediate width is large enough (the bounds check of `docs/SPEC.md` 3.6 must then cover the new
   intermediates).
5. **Difference coding of inputs** (problem 21) would narrow multiplier inputs only if the multipliers were
   bit-serial or variable width; SENTINEL's 8 × 8 lanes gain nothing from it. Not proposed.

Candidates noted for the development list: shared partial sums in the wc generator (Should, together with the
chapter 13 constant-multiplication options); FFA tiles (not proposed: Winograd-type tiles dominate for 3 × 3).
