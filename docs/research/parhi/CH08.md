## 1. Concepts, in our words

### 8.1 Strength reduction for convolution (pp. 227–228)

- Fast convolution trades strong operations (multiplications) for weak ones (additions). Example: a complex
  product needs 4 real multiplications and 2 additions directly, but 3 multiplications and 3 additions if
  `c − d` and `c + d` are precomputed for the coefficient (eqs. 8.1–8.2). Such algorithms factor the
  convolution matrix into post-additions × diagonal (the multiplications) × pre-additions.

### 8.2 Cook–Toom (pp. 228–237)

- Based on Lagrange interpolation: the product of an N-term and an L-term polynomial has L + N − 1 coefficients,
  so it is fixed by its values at L + N − 1 points. Evaluate both factors at chosen points (0, ±1, ±2, …), multiply
  the values pairwise (L + N − 1 general multiplications instead of L·N), and interpolate (Algorithm 8.2.1).
  Multiplications by the small constants of the chosen points are not counted, but they grow with the size.
- Written as `s = C·H·D·x` (eq. 8.19): C post-additions, H diagonal of precomputed filter terms, D pre-additions.
  When h is a fixed filter, the H terms are computed once offline.
- Counts: 2 × 2 convolution, 3 multiplications and 5 additions (direct: 4 and 1). 2 × 3: 4 multiplications and 12
  additions (direct: 6 and 2) (pp. 230–233).
- **Modified Cook–Toom** (Algorithm 8.2.2): compute the highest coefficient directly, interpolate the rest with
  one point fewer. 2 × 2: 3 multiplications, 3 additions; 2 × 3: 4 multiplications, 7 additions (pp. 234–237).
  Its precomputed terms include halves such as `(h0 + h1)/2` (eq. 8.33).
- Cook–Toom gets awkward for long sequences: the points must grow beyond 0, ±1, ±2, ±4 and the addition
  matrices stop being simple (p. 237).

### 8.3 Winograd (pp. 237–244)

- Based on the Chinese remainder theorem over polynomials: pick m(p) of higher degree than the product, factor it
  into coprime factors, reduce h and x modulo each factor, do the short products there, and rebuild with the CRT
  (Algorithm 8.3.1); the needed inverses come from Euclid's GCD algorithm (appendix C). The multiplication count
  depends on the degrees of the factors. Example: 2 × 3 with `m(p) = p(p − 1)(p² + 1)` needs 5 multiplications and
  11 additions. The **modified** form (m(p) of the same degree as the product, highest term computed directly)
  with `m(p) = p(p − 1)(p + 1)` needs 4 multiplications and 7 additions, the same algorithm as modified Cook–Toom
  (eqs. 8.33, 8.61).

### 8.4–8.6 Longer convolutions (pp. 244–251)

- **Iterated convolution**: nest short algorithms. A 4 × 4 convolution built from 2 × 2 inside 2 × 2 needs 9
  multiplications and 19 additions instead of 16 multiplications; the order of nesting changes the cost.
- **Cyclic convolution** (`mod pⁿ − 1`) is easy with the CRT because `pⁿ − 1` factors well: 4-point cyclic with
  5 multiplications and 15 additions (direct 16 and 12); a linear convolution can be obtained from a cyclic one
  and back (3 × 3 linear from 4 × 4 cyclic: 6 multiplications, 16 additions).
- **By inspection**: a 3 × 3 linear convolution with 6 multiplications and 10 additions that neither method gives.

### 8.7–8.8 Conclusions and problems (pp. 251–253)

- These short algorithms are the building blocks of the fast parallel FIR filters of section 9.2.2.3.

## 3. What this means for SENTINEL

The book treats 1D convolution and FIR filters; it does not treat 2D convolution layers of a CNN. Everything below
about CNN tiles is our derivation [INFERENCE].

1. **Where it would fit.** SENTINEL already computes a conv layer pool window by pool window: the four output
   positions (dy, dx) of one 2 × 2 window are consecutive (`docs/SPEC.md` 5.3), and that window is also the
   recompute unit. A 2 × 2 output tile of a 3 × 3 filter is exactly the shape a nested fast algorithm handles:
   the 2-output, 3-tap form (the transpose of the 4-multiplication 2 × 3 algorithm of eq. 8.33; transposition
   keeps the multiplication count, `CH01.md` 1.4) nested in both directions, like the iterated convolution of
   section 8.4, gives 4 × 4 = 16 multiplications per window and input channel instead of 4 × 9 = 36, i.e. 2.25
   times fewer for 3 × 3 conv layers.
2. **What it would cost.** Per window and input channel, a 4 × 4 input patch is transformed with additions only
   (the same for every lane, so the broadcast stays); each lane multiplies 16 transformed activations by 16
   precomputed transformed weights and keeps 16 accumulators instead of 1; after the last input channel an output
   transform (additions) gives the 4 outputs, then requant and max-pool as now. Weight memory grows by 16/9 and
   gets wider; activations and weights after the transforms need about 11–12 bits instead of 8, so the multipliers
   grow (a fit for the unused DSP slices, `CH03.md`).
3. **Bit-exactness is possible.** The halves in H (eq. 8.33) can be removed by storing doubled filter terms and
   dividing the final integer result by 2 per dimension; the division is exact because the true result is an
   integer. With enough accumulator bits the outputs equal direct convolution, so the golden model can stay as it
   is. This would need its own bounds check like `docs/SPEC.md` 3.6.
4. **Checksums still work in principle.** The checksum relations are sums over output channels (lanes); the tile
   transforms act on positions within one channel and are linear, so they commute with the checksum. Transformed
   checksum weights are the same sums of transformed weights, and the identities hold per output after the output
   transform, where correction per output position stays possible. A fault in one transform-domain product spreads
   to up to 4 outputs of the same lane and window, which the per-output correction and the window recompute cover.
   The input transform would be outside the checksum's protection, like the activation broadcast today
   (`docs/SPEC.md`, checksum scope), so it would have to run in the safe clock domain.
5. **Schedule.** This is a new engine datapath, memory layout and test set. Against G1 (13 Dec 2026), the freeze
   (17 Jan 2027) and board bring-up in December–January, it does not fit as a deliverable. It fits as an evaluated
   option in the report. `docs/PRIOR_ART.md` already lists work on Winograd convolution and fault tolerance
   (Xue et al., 2022), so any originality claim would need a check there first.

Candidates noted for the development list: Winograd-style 2 × 2 tiles for 3 × 3 layers (Could; report as an
evaluated option with the 2.25× multiplication count; not before the freeze).
